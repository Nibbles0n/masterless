Reordered Muon at 4 Bits

Two ideas from the owner, tested. Can Muon's momentum act as an accumulator, so that a 4-bit weight moves one grid step only once enough small pushes have added up? And do bigger batches with bigger learning rates make 4-bit rounding hurt less?

25 October 2026 · 93 training runs on one free Colab T4 (fp16) · every number comes from runs/m6, runs/batch_lr and report/data

The short version

  1. Milestone 6 failed, clearly. Reordered Muon with 4-bit weights and the leftover kept in the momentum ended at 2.05, against 1.75 for plain random rounding (m3) and 1.65 for 4-bit with a float copy (m2). All three pass lines fail.
  2. The cause is known. The momentum hands a leftover back over about 20 steps, so a weight moves only after about 10 gaps of wanted movement have piled up. A one-weight simulation shows it lagging 9 gaps behind for good.
  3. 8-bit momentum is free. Storing the momentum in 8 bits changed the loss by +0.001 ± 0.006. That supports a roughly 2-byte-per-weight budget once a rule that works is found.
  4. Reordering costs even with float weights (+0.069 against Muon), so this order of Muon is a dead end.
  5. Bigger learning rates make 4-bit rounding hurt more, not less. At every batch size, raising the rate widened the m3 − m1 gap. Bigger batches allow bigger rates and reach the same loss in 2–4 times fewer steps, but no lower loss.
  6. Keep small scale blocks, in 8 bits. One scale per matrix doubled one-off rounding damage, for a saving of about 4% of memory.

1The idea behind milestone 6

A 4-bit weight can only sit on one of 15 values. Without a float copy, a step smaller than the gap between two values is lost unless something remembers it. ECO's trick for Adam keeps the unused part (the leftover) in the momentum, and it comes back out on later steps. That works for Adam because each weight's step depends only on that weight's own momentum. In milestone 4, Muon broke it: Muon squashes the whole momentum matrix before stepping, and only about 70% of a fed-back leftover came out pointing the right way.

Milestone 6 swaps Muon's two operations, so the step becomes a plain multiple of the momentum again:

normal Muon M ← 0.95·M + 0.05·G step = lr · NS(M) (average, then squash) reordered A ← 0.95·A + 0.05·NS(G) step = lr · A (squash, then average) leftover e = wanted weight − stored 4-bit weight A ← A + (1 − 1/0.95) · e / lr (e comes back out over the next steps)

Here NS is Muon's squashing step (Newton–Schulz), which gives every direction of a matrix the same strength. With nearest rounding, a weight stays put until its pushes add up past half a gap, then moves one level. That is the owner's Bop-style "tick when the accumulator crosses a threshold", with no extra memory: the momentum holds the leftover.

Why it failed

The momentum does act as an accumulator, but it hands back what it holds slowly: each step uses only about 1/20 of it (that is what 0.95 momentum means). The rounding looks only at one step's share. So a weight moves one level only when a single step's push passes half a gap. By then about 20 times as much, roughly 9 to 10 gaps, is waiting inside the momentum. The weight ends up trailing where it should be by about 9 gaps, permanently. A separate leftover buffer (milestone 4c) doesn't have this problem, because it applies the whole leftover on the very next step.

Two lessons. First, the momentum can't double as the leftover store this way, because it releases what it holds too slowly. A Bop-style tick needs a counter that the rounding reads in full every step, like milestone 4c's buffer. Second, the reordering itself is worse than Muon even with float weights (+0.069). A likely reason (not measured): squashing each noisy gradient on its own gives every direction equal strength, noise included, so averaging afterwards can no longer tell steady directions from noisy ones.

3Bigger batches with bigger learning rates

Study B showed that bigger batches alone don't help: with the learning rate unchanged, they just get less far on the same data. The owner's follow-up: maybe bigger steps suffer less from rounding, and bigger batches allow bigger steps. So every learning rate (Muon's and AdamW's) was multiplied by 2, 4 or 8, at 1×, 2×, 4× and 8× the batch of 12. Every run reads the same 1.5 million characters.

So the owner's idea holds only in a weak form. Bigger batches let the learning rate grow, and with a matched rate the rounding damage at equal progress is about a fifth smaller. But a bigger step is never safer on its own: the larger the step, the bigger the gap. For the goal of cheaper training, the useful part is speed. A 4× batch at twice the rate reached m3's best loss in a quarter of the steps.

4The owner's other questions

Should we just use one scale per matrix?

A scale is the ruler a 4-bit code is measured with: weight = code × scale. The codes run only from −6 to 6, and real weights are around 0.02, so at least one ruler per matrix is needed. With one ruler per matrix, a single large weight sets the ruler for everyone, and most small weights land on 0 or ±0.5. Smaller blocks give each group of weights its own ruler.

So no, not yet. Keep one ruler per 16 weights, stored in 8 bits. One thing remains untested: in training without a float copy, a block's ruler changes whenever its largest weight changes, which nudges all 16 weights at once. One ruler per matrix would change far less often. That makes a specific, cheap test for later.

Has anyone trained with FP8 weights and 8-bit momentum?

We haven't tested it here. Outside, the pieces exist, but not all together:

FP8 weights with no copy plus an 8-bit momentum is about 2 bytes per weight, against 16 for normal AdamW training. That count covers only the weights and the optimizer: gradients and activations need memory too, unless gradients are applied and dropped layer by layer as the backward pass goes.

5What to test next

A · Milestone 4c with small buffers about 2.1–2.6 bytes

Normal Muon, 4-bit weights, no float copy, and milestone 4c's separate leftover buffer, the rule that works in the simulation. The leftover is never bigger than half a gap, so it fits in 4 or 8 bits, and the momentum goes to 8 bits (free, per this round). Milestone 4c was the best no-copy Muon arm so far: 1.683 on the T4, 0.037 behind milestone 2.

B · FP8 weights, no copy, 8-bit momentum about 2 bytes

The owner's question, tested directly: milestone 3 with an 8-bit float grid instead of the 4-bit one, plus 8-bit momentum. A 4-bit weight with a 4-bit leftover is an 8-bit number in disguise, so A and B spend about the same memory in two different ways. Testing both shows which way is better.

  • A fixed version of the accumulator idea, which would compare the whole stored amount with the gap, was not tried.
