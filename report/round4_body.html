Two Bytes per Weight

Two ways to spend about 2 bytes per weight on training, with no full-precision copy of the weights. One keeps a 4-bit weight with a 4-bit leftover buffer. The other keeps an 8-bit (FP8) weight. Both store the momentum in 8 bits. Then the FP8 model is rounded down to 4 bits after training, to see whether that beats training in 4 bits from the start. Last, the owner's ticker: a 4-bit weight that moves one step only when its carried leftover crosses half the gap.

26 October 2026 · 9 training runs, 4 evaluations, on free Colab T4s (fp16) · baselines from round 3's runs on the same kind of T4 · every number comes from runs/m7, runs/ticker, runs/m6 and report/data

The short version

  1. The ticker works: 4-bit weights trained with no float copy, level with keeping one. 4-bit weights, a 4-bit carry measured in units of each weight's own gap, and 8-bit momentum ended at 1.650, against 1.646 for milestone 2, which keeps a full 32-bit copy. That is 2.28 bytes per weight against 8, within 0.03 of milestone 2 in every seed, and the best no-copy 4-bit result in the project. It was a measure-only study, so it still needs a formal pass line on fresh runs.
  2. FP8 weights with no float copy work too. FP8 weights plus 8-bit momentum ended at 1.652, only 0.021 behind normal float training (pass line met in all 3 seeds) and level with milestone 2, which keeps a full float copy. That is 2.28 bytes per weight against 8.
  3. The 4-bit + 4-bit buffer version failed its line. It ended at 1.712, 0.066 behind milestone 2 (the line was 0.03). It is still 0.043 better than plain 4-bit with no buffer (m3), so the small buffer does help, but it lost 0.029 against milestone 4c's 16-bit buffer.
  4. Training in FP8 and then rounding to 4 bits beat that buffer version by 0.040 (1.672), but the ticker beats the rounded FP8 model by 0.022, in every seed. So how the 4-bit leftover is kept matters more than how many bits it has.
  5. The math, in one line: random rounding's error grows like the square root of the total distance a weight travels, back and forth. Real Muon weights travel about 183 gaps to move 24, so the noise is half the signal. The ticker cancels that noise instead of paying for it with more bits.

1What was tested

Training normally keeps each weight as a 32-bit number, plus a 32-bit momentum: 8 bytes per weight before gradients and activations. Without a float copy, the stored weight is the low-bit number, and every step has to round the new weight onto the grid. Steps smaller than the gap between grid values get lost unless something remembers them. Milestone 4c showed that a separate leftover buffer works: it holds what the rounding threw away and adds it back on the next step. But it cost 2 bytes on its own.

A 4-bit weight with a 4-bit leftover is really an 8-bit number split in two. So this round asks which way of spending the same 8 bits is better:

Run 1 · 4-bit + 4-bit buffer + 8-bit momentum

  1. Muon step (average, then squash)
  2. add the stored leftover
  3. round at random to the 4-bit grid
  4. new leftover → 4-bit buffer

2.53 bytes per weight (2.16 with 8-bit scales)

Run 2 · FP8 + 8-bit momentum

  1. Muon step (average, then squash)
  2. round at random to the FP8 grid
  3. leftover thrown away

2.28 bytes per weight (2.09 with 8-bit scales)

Eval 3 · FP8 model, rounded to 4 bits after

  1. take run 2's finished weights
  2. round each to the nearest 4-bit value, once
  3. evaluate; no more training

the usual way models are shrunk today

All three end up evaluated the same way: the loss on the same 256 stretches of held-out text, in nats per character (lower is better). The two 4-bit models (run 1 and eval 3) use exactly the same 4-bit format, so they can be compared directly. The references come from round 3, run on the same kind of T4: m1 (normal Muon, float weights), m2 (4-bit weights with a float copy kept), m3 (4-bit, no copy, no buffer) and m4c (4-bit with a 16-bit leftover buffer).

Details that matter

  • 4-bit grid: the E2M1 format (values 0, ±0.5, ±1, ±1.5, ±2, ±3, ±4, ±6) times one scale per 16 weights, as in milestones 2–6.
  • FP8 grid: the E4M3 format, which has 3 bits of precision at every size (neighbouring values 6–12% apart), times one scale per 16 weights, so each block's largest weight sits on 448, the format's largest value.
  • 4-bit buffer: whole numbers from −7 to 7 times one scale per 16 leftovers. 8-bit momentum: −127 to 127 times one scale per 128 values. Both are rounded at random, so small values survive on average instead of always rounding to zero.
  • Only Muon's four big matrices per layer are made low-bit (99% of the weights). The embeddings, norms and output layer stay in 32 bits with AdamW, as in every milestone.
  • Same model, data, 2000 steps and learning rate (0.01) as milestones 1–4. Nothing was tuned for the new runs.

3What milestone 7 alone suggested

Overturned by section 4. This section is as written after milestone 7. The ticker later showed that a 4-bit weight plus a 4-bit leftover can match FP8, when the leftover is kept the ticker's way.

Why did one FP8 number beat this 4-bit number plus a 4-bit leftover? Both use 8 bits. Here is a likely reason; it was not measured. The FP8 grid has fine steps everywhere: neighbouring values are 6–12% apart, at every size. So most of Muon's small steps survive random rounding with little noise. The 4-bit grid's steps are coarse (up to a third of a block's largest weight), so it leans on the buffer to remember small moves. A 4-bit buffer is itself coarse: its steps are 1/7 of the block's largest leftover. Re-rounding it every step adds noise that doesn't shrink as the learning rate does. That fits the curves: the 4-bit run keeps pace while steps are big, then falls behind as they shrink.

Why does rounding after training work so well here? One-off rounding to nearest makes errors that don't pile up. Training in 4 bits makes a rounding decision on every weight at every step, and the noise adds up over 2000 steps. This matches what the field already does: train at higher precision, then shrink the finished model.

What this means for training cheaply. FP8 weights with no float copy and 8-bit momentum cost about 2.1–2.3 bytes per weight for the weights and optimizer, against 8 for float Muon and 16 for normal AdamW training. On this small model they came within 0.021 of float Muon and level with 4-bit training that keeps a float copy. That is about a 3.5× cut in that part of memory, for a small loss in quality. If the end goal is a 4-bit model, rounding after training is the safe route, though the ticker (next section) did better.

4The ticker

The owner's idea: give each 4-bit weight a counter. Add every step's push to it, and tick the weight one level up or down only when the counter passes half the gap to the next value, carrying the remainder. On a 4-bit grid the gaps differ in size (0.5 near zero, 2 between 4 and 6), so each weight gets its own thresholds, which rounding to the nearest value gives for free.

target = stored 4-bit weight + carry − lr · Muon update new weight = the nearest 4-bit value to the target (ticks once the carry passes half a gap) new carry = target − new weight (always within ±½ of its side's gap) stored as = a whole number from −7 to 7, meaning k/14 of that gap (rounded at random)

Two things differ from milestone 7's 4-bit buffer run. The weight is rounded to nearest, not at random, so the weight itself adds no noise. And the carry is measured in units of its own weight's gap, so a small weight's carry is as finely stored as a big one's. The buffer run used one ruler per 16 leftovers, set by the largest. The carry needs no ruler of its own, so the memory is the same 2.28 bytes as FP8.

Is it "just 8-bit weights, then rounded"? No, and the numbers show it. The 4-bit weight plus a carry in sixteenths of a gap holds about as many bits as FP8, but the forward pass sees the 4-bit weight on every step. So the model learns to work with the exact 4-bit values it will ship with, and no rounding is left for the end. That is a likely reason it beat the FP8-then-round route by 0.022 (not measured). Another likely one: the carry's resolution is even inside each 4-bit gap, exactly where the decision to tick is made, while FP8's spacing follows the weight's size.

Caveats. This was a measure-only study, with its questions written down before the run. Meeting the milestone 4 line here doesn't count as the paper test, which needs that line set as a pass line in advance and run on fresh seeds. The two differences from milestone 7 were changed together, so which one does the work isn't known. And everything is still the small model at 2000 steps; the longer run in study L is where plain 4-bit fell apart.

5Why low bits are hard, for any optimizer

This is the math behind every result so far, in one idea. Each step, the optimizer wants to move a weight by a small amount: the push. Measured on a real Muon run (report/path_probe.py), a typical push is 0.17 of a 4-bit gap at the peak learning rate and 0.017 at the end. So nearly every step asks for less than one gap.

Random rounding gets the average right. A push of 0.17 gaps moves the weight a whole gap 17% of the time and not at all otherwise. But each step adds a little randomness. Its variance (the square of its typical size) is about push × gap. Variances add up over steps, so after a whole run:

typical error ≈ √( gap × total distance travelled )

The key words are distance travelled: the sum of every step's size, back and forth, not the net movement. Real training zig-zags. In the measured Muon run, a weight travelled 183 gaps in all to end up 24 gaps from where it started. That predicts an error of about √183 ≈ 13.5 gaps, against a useful movement of 24. Half the signal is noise. That is why milestone 3 (random rounding, no memory) lost 0.12.

So there are only three ways to shrink the error:

  1. A smaller gap: more bits. FP8 does this. It worked (milestone 7), but it is what you called "just increasing precision".
  2. Remember the leftover: a carry. The ticker and milestone 4c do this. With a perfect carry the error stays under half a gap for good, but the carry needs enough bits to hold steps of 1/60 of a gap at the end of training (the 0.017 above). That is about 6 bits beyond the 4-bit grid. With fewer bits, the carry's own rounding becomes the noise.
  3. Travel less for the same net movement: less zig-zag. Nothing so far has tried this. Muon (like Adam) takes full-size steps every time, even when the gradient is mostly noise, so its path is long: 7.6 gaps travelled for every 1 gained. If a weight's steps were shrunk when their direction keeps flipping, and kept full when it is steady, the path would get shorter while the net movement stayed about the same. The noise would fall with the square root of the path.

The third way is the one that changes Muon itself rather than the number format, which is what you asked for. It is also what Bop does in spirit for 1-bit weights: it only flips a weight when the momentum agrees for long enough. A concrete test: scale each weight's step by how consistent its recent sign has been, and measure distance travelled, net movement and final loss with random-rounding 4-bit weights.

  • The buffer was rounded at random; nearest rounding, an 8-bit buffer, and FP8 with a buffer were not tried.
  • Rounding after training used plain nearest rounding only, with no fine-tune and no smarter rounding.
  • Learning rate 0.01 for both arms, untuned; scales stored in 32 bits during training (8-bit scales were counted in the bytes but not run).

  • Everything is the small CPU-preset model (about 0.8 million parameters) on Shakespeare characters, 2000 steps, fp16 on a T4. FP8 and 4-bit are simulated: the numbers are stored exactly as the formats allow, but the matrix multiplies run in fp16, so there is no speed measurement.
