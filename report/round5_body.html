The Zig-Zag Tax

Storing weights in few bits charges a tax on every move a weight makes, back and forth, while training only gains from where the weight ends up. Muon is built to move every direction equally far, so it pays the most tax for the least gain. This page states that precisely, measures it on a real run, and turns it into a design problem with one free knob.

26 October 2026 · theory plus one measurement run of milestone 1 (Muon, float weights, CPU preset, seed 1) at three momentum settings · source: report/zigzag_theory.md, report/zigzag_probe.py

The short version

  1. One rule predicts all the rounding damage: drift ≈ √(rounding spacing × path), where the path counts every move back and forth. It matches the simulation within about 10% for plain 4-bit, an FP8-like grid and the ticker. Each extra bit cuts the drift by only 1.41 times. Halving the zig-zag is worth as much as one extra bit.
  2. Under Muon the path is fixed by the learning rate, not by the gradient. Weights travelled 179, 183 and 186 gaps at momentum 0.9, 0.95 and 0.99. Momentum only changed how far they got: the zig-zag factor was 11.1, 7.6 and 3.1. Pure gradient noise predicts these ratios (1.43 and 2.27; measured 1.47 and 2.45). Steps were positively correlated from one to the next (0.57 to 0.92), so the zig-zag is noise, not bouncing.
  3. Turning up momentum is not the fix. At 0.99 float training got worse (1.690 against 1.622) because the average lags, and the path, which is what plain rounding pays for, did not get shorter.
  4. Muon spends its movement evenly, but its progress is concentrated. About half of Muon's downhill progress (35–93% over training) comes from the momentum's 8 strongest directions out of 128, yet Muon moves all 128 equally far. The other 120 directions are mostly noise, boosted to full size.
  5. Simple changes to Muon's step get 1.4 to 6 times more downhill progress per unit of path: keeping only the 8 strongest directions (1.5–3.8×), taking step sizes from the momentum (2.1–3.9×), skipping small moves (1.4×), or moving only where this batch agrees (1.8–6.1×). Twice the progress per unit of path is worth one extra bit. This is a one-step, first-order measure: only a training run can show whether it survives over a whole run.
  6. The design problem in one line: choose the step to minimise (first-order loss change) + λ × Σ (rounding spacing × |move|), with Muon's own size limit. At high precision this turns back into Muon. Its one knob λ prices rounding against progress.

1The one rule everything follows from

Random rounding stores a value on a grid with spacing r. If the wanted value sits a fraction f of the way to the next grid point, it stores the upper point with probability f. That is right on average, but each rounding adds a spread (variance) of r²·f·(1 − f). For a small step δ, f = |δ|/r, so each rounding adds about r·|δ|, never more than r²/4. The roundings are independent, so their variances add:

drift from the float path ≈ √(r × path), where path = the sum of every |move|, back and forth.
drift ÷ net movement = √(r ÷ net) × √Z, where Z = path ÷ net is the zig-zag factor (1 = a straight line).

On a real Muon run the weights travel about 183 gaps to move 24 net, so Z ≈ 7.6 and plain 4-bit rounding drifts √183 ≈ 13.5 gaps against a signal of 24. More bits help only slowly: the drift falls with the square root of r, so each extra bit cuts it by a factor of 1.41. Halving Z buys as much as one extra bit.

The rule ignores the pull-back. Where the loss curves up, the gradient pulls a misplaced weight back, and the drift settles instead of growing. Most directions in a network are nearly flat, and there it keeps growing. Side study B agrees: plain 4-bit's damage tracked how far training had gone, not the batch size.

2Where the zig-zag comes from

  1. Gradient noise. Each weight's gradient is a steady signal μ plus noise σ. Momentum averages about 1/(1 − β) steps and shrinks the noise to σ·√((1 − β)/(1 + β)): 0.23σ at β = 0.9, 0.16σ at 0.95, 0.07σ at 0.99. If steps follow the momentum, Z ≈ 0.8 × (momentum's noise ÷ μ). Prediction: going from β = 0.95 to 0.99 cuts Z by 2.2 times, if μ holds still in the meantime.
  2. Bouncing. In a sharply curved direction a fixed-size step overshoots the bottom and the weight hops side to side. Its sign: one step correlates negatively with the next. Noise passed through momentum gives a positive correlation instead.
  3. Muon spreads its movement. Newton–Schulz sets every direction of the momentum to the same strength. The strong directions carry the signal and the weak ones are mostly noise, so Muon blows noise up to full size. In maths: Muon is the steepest-descent step under the spectral norm (the most downhill step among those whose largest stretch is η; Bernstein & Newhouse 2024). Low-bit storage bills the sum of |moves| (the L1 norm), and steepest descent under that norm does the opposite of Muon: it puts all movement into the few weights with the strongest signal.

4The design problem, as one line of maths

Plain Muon solves: choose the step δ that makes ⟨G, δ⟩ (the first-order change in loss, G = momentum) as negative as possible, subject to ‖δ‖spectral ≤ η. The low-bit version adds the bill from section 1:

choose δ to minimise ⟨G, δ⟩ + λ · Σi ri · |δi|, subject to ‖δ‖spectral ≤ η

ri = the rounding spacing at weight i (its gap for plain rounding, the carry's spacing for the ticker, about |w|/8 for FP8). λ = how much one unit of rounding spread costs in loss: unknown, the one knob to tune.

  • Without the spectral limit the bill is a lasso (L1) penalty, and its exact answer per weight is the soft threshold: shrink every move toward zero by λ·ri, and moves smaller than that become zero.
  • As the bits go up (r → 0) the problem turns back into plain Muon. Any method built from it keeps Muon's behaviour at high precision.
  • With both terms there is no closed-form answer. Two cheap approximations: Muon first, then soft-threshold each weight; or shrink the momentum's weak directions before orthogonalising (fix 2.3 at its source), then threshold.
  • G is noisy. A weight with a tiny true signal looks like noise, and the threshold stops exactly those. The price is that weights with small but real signals move later. λ sets that trade.

Low-rank orthogonalised updates exist already, for example Dion (2025), built to save communication between GPUs. As far as I know nobody has tested them for low-bit weights.

5A first test, for you to shape

The theory makes a prediction that can fail: a fix that shortens the path should help plain random rounding (m3) a lot, and the ticker only a little (section 1). Testing both checks the theory as well as the fix. A small first test on the CPU preset, seeds 1–3, on a T4:

  1. Pick one or two candidate fixes. My suggestion: Muon on the k strongest directions (k = 8, 16), which gained on both measures, and the soft threshold, which is the exact answer to the bill when the spectral limit is left out. Its λ is the one knob.
  2. For each, run three arms: m3 + fix, the ticker + fix, and float Muon + fix. The float arm checks that the fix alone does not hurt training.
  3. Measure the path, the net movement, the drift from a float twin, and the final loss.
  4. A possible pass line, to fix in PLAN.md before running: m3 + fix closes at least half of m3's 0.117 gap to m1, so m3 + fix ≤ m1 + 0.06 in each seed. Float Muon + fix stays within 0.02 of m1.

The open design questions are yours:

  • How to set λ: fixed, tied to the learning rate, or tied to the grid spacing r so the rule turns into Muon at high precision.
  • Whether to shrink before or after orthogonalising.
  • Whether the ticker's carry can serve as the free confidence signal.

6Not tested

  • Everything measured here is one float Muon run (seed 1, CPU preset, 2000 steps, on this machine's CPU in fp32). Path lengths are in units of each weight's 4-bit gap at the start.
  • The progress measures are first-order and one-step: they say how much each step goes downhill right now, per unit of path. They do not show which rule trains better over a whole run, because Muon's even spreading also helps it handle curvature. Only a training run can settle that.
  • The "true gradient" is the mean of 64 fresh batches. Its own noise does not bias the progress numbers, but it makes each one noisy, so single checkpoints should not be over-read.
  • The pull-back from curvature is left out of the rule. Muon's fixed step size makes it hard to model.
