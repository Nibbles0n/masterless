RL in 4 Bits: Level with DeepSeek's Recipe at a Third of the Memory

Newest: the fair comparison for RL. A model that is served on 4-bit chips has to be made of 4-bit weights only, so the right comparison is the way such models are trained today. DeepSeek-V4 trains its 4-bit weights through a full-precision copy (fp32: 32 bits per weight): the copy takes each update, and the 4-bit weights are re-rounded from it at every step. We ran that recipe, setup (d), on the same model, format, start and settings as ours, three seeds. Ours ends 2.0 points ahead (68.6% against 66.6%; 95% interval +0.5 to +3.6), and the pass line written before the runs (lower end above −3) is met. Ours keeps 2.78 bytes per weight for training, the copy recipe 8.75.

We had predicted the two would end within a point of each other. Ours was ahead on every seed, but the copy recipe's seeds spread widely (68.1, 67.3, 64.3), so we call it level, not better. Both trail normal 16-bit training, mostly because rounding the starting model to 4 bits costs 6.5 points before any training.

Before that: a GPTQ start for RL. Rounding Qwen3.5-0.8B to 4 bits with GPTQ instead of to nearest lifts its starting score on the exam from 48.1% to 51.2% (+3.0 ± 2.8 points; the 16-bit model scores 54.7%). That is about half of what plain rounding costs, as predicted. We measured the start only and ran no RL from it, so whether RL keeps that lead is untested (section G).

Before that: GPTQ, the careful way to round a trained model, narrows one claim. On GPT-2 small (continued training), models trained in 16 bits and rounded with GPTQ are still 0.009–0.012 behind ours. From scratch (45M), GPTQ wins back 98% of what plain rounding lost and ends level with ours (+0.003 ± 0.002). As written before the runs, the paper now says that from scratch ours beats only plain rounding.

After the outside review: ECO in its own setting loses clearly. ECO run the way its paper runs it (AdamW, random rounding, full-precision moments, 8.75 bytes per weight) ends 0.080 behind ours on GPT-2 small, in every one of 3 seeds. The pass line, written before the runs, needed only about 0.003.

The short version

  1. Ours matches the way 4-bit models are trained today. Against DeepSeek's recipe, a full-precision copy behind the 4-bit weights, ours ends 2.0 points ahead with a third of the training memory. P5d passes. It is ahead on every seed, but by too little, given how much the copy recipe's seeds vary, to call it better (section 1).
  2. Both 4-bit setups trail normal 16-bit training, and P5a fails, as the rule says. Ours ends 3.7 points behind (interval −5.2 to −2.2, line −4); the copy recipe ends 5.7 behind. Rounding the model to 4 bits costs 6.5 points before training starts, and 200 steps win back about two fifths of that.
  3. LoRA ends 2.6 points above ours, but only with a 16-bit add-on. That add-on means the served model cannot run on 4-bit arithmetic alone. Merged into the weights and rounded to 4 bits, every weight lands back on its start and the model scores 47.7%.
  4. GPTQ: ours still wins in continued training, and ties from scratch. GPTQ recovers about half of plain rounding's loss on GPT-2 small, which is not enough to catch ours. On the 45M model trained from scratch it recovers 98%, and bf16 training plus GPTQ ends level with ours at 9% more memory. The paper's claim against "train in 16 bits, then round" now holds from scratch only against plain rounding (section G).
  5. ECO with AdamW is now tested too, and loses. It is ECO's own setting, with three times our memory, and it ends 0.080 behind ours in every seed (section 0).
  6. Compute: the P5 round used 23.6 units; the ECO-AdamW runs 20.2, about 10 of them wasted on an idle session; GPTQ 9.5; the GPTQ start for RL 1.8; setup (d) 27.6, about 11 of them lost when a session ended in the middle of two runs, which were run again. GGPTQ: rounding a 16-bit model carefully

The paper's "train in 16 bits, then round to 4 bits" baselines rounded each weight to its nearest 4-bit value. GPTQ is the standard careful way: it rounds one column of a weight matrix at a time and shifts that column's rounding error onto the columns not yet rounded, choosing the shift so the layer's outputs on 128 sample text windows change as little as possible. We ran it on our exact 4-bit format on four models: the pretrained GPT-2 (P1's start), the float Muon and bf16-SR models of P1 (seed 1), and the bf16-SR model of P2 (seed 1). The retrained models match the logged runs exactly, and every GPTQ weight lies on the 4-bit grid. The prediction and what each result would mean were written in the plan before the runs.

The RL starting model. Every 4-bit RL run (P5) started from Qwen3.5-0.8B rounded to nearest. Rounded with GPTQ instead (calibrated on 128 GSM8K training answers, the same exam setup, one A100), the start scores 51.2% against 48.1%, a gain of 3.0 ± 2.8 points; the same run re-scored the 16-bit and rounded-to-nearest models and matched the logged 54.7% and 48.1% exactly. Ours ended RL at 68.6% (mean of 3 seeds) from the 48.1% start. A GPTQ start would begin 3 points higher, but no RL was run from it.

What ours still has that bf16 training plus GPTQ does not: less memory (2.78 bytes per weight against 3.03), the lead in continued training, and weights that are already the 4-bit weights while training, which is what lets RL (P5) sample answers from the same model it trains.

0P1e: ECO in its own setting

The outside review asked the fairest question: ECO was built for Adam, not Muon, so does it lose only because we ran it with Muon? P1e runs ECO's Adam version as its paper does: the same 4-bit grid as ours, random rounding (which the ECO paper finds best), the rounding error fed into Adam's first moment, and both of Adam's moments kept in full precision. That is 8.75 bytes per weight against ours 2.78. Its learning rate is the one the 4-bit float copy with AdamW uses; it was not tuned for ECO, as written in the plan before the runs. The test: pretrained GPT-2 small, 100 million more tokens, 3 seeds. Lower loss is better.

For scale: against the same 4-bit model trained with AdamW and a full-precision copy (12 bytes per weight), ECO-AdamW is 0.084 behind, and ours is 0.0040 behind.

1P5: RL in 4 bits

Each setup trains Qwen3.5-0.8B for 200 steps, three seeds each. At each step it tries 8 answers to each of 32 practice questions and learns from which ones were right. Then it sits the 1,319-question exam. Two setups end with a model made of 4-bit weights only, the kind 4-bit chips can serve directly:

  • (c) ours: every weight in 4 bits, with an 8-bit carry and no copy (2.78 bytes per weight).
  • (d) DeepSeek's recipe: every weight in 4 bits, re-rounded at each step from a full-precision copy that takes the updates (8.75 bytes per weight). Same start, format, optimizer and settings as ours.

Two more are references that need 16-bit weights when served: (a) normal training in 16 bits from the unrounded model, and (b) a frozen 4-bit model with a small 16-bit LoRA add-on.

2Why rounded LoRA scores its start

What was measured

  • At the end of LoRA seeds 2 and 3, the add-on was merged into the weights and rounded to the same 4-bit grid ours uses, then the exam was taken again.
  • Share of 4-bit values that changed: 0, on both seeds.
  • Add-on size: on average 0.5% of the gap between neighbouring 4-bit values (root mean square 1.8e-5).
  • Score: 47.6% and 47.8%, against a 48.1% start (−0.4 ± 2.0: no change).

What it means

  • LoRA's 23-point gain is real, but it is stored entirely in a 16-bit add-on, not in the 4-bit weights.
  • Ours moves the 4-bit values themselves: 0.11% of them change on each step (the copy recipe: 0.033%). What it learns is in the model you serve.
  • The prediction we wrote before the runs said "most weights round back to their start". In fact all of them did.
  • Not tested: other ways to bring LoRA's gain into 4 bits, such as training the block scales (Scale-QLoRA).

3P2: GPT-2 from scratch in 4 bits

A 45-million-weight GPT-2 model is trained from random weights on 0.9 billion tokens of web text: 27,466 steps, about 20 tokens per weight. Ours is compared with the same 4-bit model trained with a full-precision copy (8 bytes per weight), over three seeds. The score is the validation loss: lower is better.

The learning rate was picked from three, with short runs of the float copy: 0.04, the top of the grid (loss 4.268 against 4.301 and 4.306). A larger rate might be better still. The same rate is used for every arm.

4What this does not show

  • GPTQ: one seed of each baseline, one GPTQ setting (no column reordering, no search over scales, 128 calibration windows). A stronger version might do better still. RL from a GPTQ start was not run.
  • P1e: one model, continued training at a small learning rate, ECO-AdamW not tuned. From scratch, where steps are larger, ECO may do better; this is proposed to reviewers as an extension.
  • Setup (d) uses our 4-bit format (blocks of 16, full-precision scales) and our optimizer, not DeepSeek's exact format (MXFP4: blocks of 32, power-of-two scales), its 8-bit arithmetic or its optimizer.
  • P5: one model (0.8B), one task, 200 steps. Whether ours keeps closing the gap with longer training, or with a short healing run before RL, is untested.
  • P5's peak GPU memory is set by vLLM's reserved share and the activations (27–31 GB for normal, ours and the copy recipe, 24 GB for LoRA). LoRA does use less memory than ours, since its frozen weights need no gradients or carry. The weight and optimizer saving against full training is measured in P6.
  • LoRA's learning rate (1e-5) was not tuned; neither was ours beyond the safety rule.
  • The rounded-LoRA test covers seeds 2 and 3 only (seed 1 ran before it was added).
  • P2: one model size, learning rate at the top of its grid, the batch size not tuned, one seed of bf16-SR.
  • The answer writer (vLLM) holds a 16-bit copy of the 4-bit values; a true 4-bit answer writer is not measured.
