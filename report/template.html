Masterless Muon at 4 Bits

What ran, what broke, and why. Can Muon train weights that live only on a 4-bit grid, with no float copy kept, if the rounding error is fed back the way ECO does it? Not yet. This page shows why from several angles, and what it would take to make 4-bit stable before going to ternary.

25 October 2026 · 47 scored training runs (29 on this machine's CPU, 18 on a free T4) and 8 instrumented re-runs · every number comes from runs/ and report/data

The short version

  1. Muon is much better than AdamW on this model: 1.628 against 1.915 nats per character, the same in all 3 seeds.
  2. A 4-bit grid is cheap if you keep a float copy: +0.019 (milestone 2).
  3. Dropping the float copy costs six times more: +0.117 (milestone 3). The cost keeps growing as the learning rate falls.
  4. ECO's fix works for Adam and backfires for Muon. ECO feeds the rounding error into the momentum. For Adam, 4-bit training ends only 0.013 behind full precision. For Muon, the loss gets worse than with no fix at all (1.898). Newton-Schulz, Muon's orthogonalising step, throws away about half of the fed-back error and bends the rest.
  5. Feeding the error in after Newton-Schulz nearly passes (+0.027 against the +0.03 line, 1 of 3 seeds inside it). But it needs a separate 2-byte error store, and that store plus the 4-bit weight is just a float copy in two pieces.
  6. The root problem is that a Muon step is tiny next to the grid. It moves a weight by 0.1–7% of the gap between two grid values. That fraction has to be stored somewhere, or replaced by noise. Adam's momentum can hold it. Muon's cannot, because Newton-Schulz rewrites the momentum's size.

What the follow-ups added

  1. Spreading the rounding error over the matrix makes things worse (milestone 5: +0.088 against m3, every seed, as an offline check predicted). Without a float copy, each weight is already within a tiny step of a grid point, where random rounding is nearly exact. Spreading pushes weights to the middle of a gap, where it is noisiest (section 10).
  2. FP8 block scales, as in NVFP4, barely matter: +0.008, the size of the seed-to-seed spread (section 11).
  3. Trained 5× longer, the picture changes. Muon's lead over AdamW shrinks from 0.29 to 0.034, so it was mostly speed. m3 is faster than AdamW early on but is overtaken around step 3,250 and ends 0.13 behind: no-copy 4-bit has a higher floor (section 12).

1What was run

One small language model was trained on one small dataset, many times. Each milestone changes exactly one thing about how the model's big matrices are stored or updated, and is judged against the milestone before it.

Model
A GPT-style transformer: 4 layers, width 128, 4 attention heads, 64-character context, 0.80 million weights. Each layer has four big matrices, qkv (128→384), proj (128→128), fc (128→512) and fc_out (512→128). Together they hold 786k weights.
Data
Tiny Shakespeare: 1.1 million characters, 65 different symbols. The first 90% is for training, the last 10% for validation.
Training
2000 steps of 12 sequences × 64 characters (1.5 million characters, about 1.5 passes over the training text). The learning rate warms up for 100 steps, then follows a cosine curve down to 10% of its peak.
Score
Validation cross-entropy in nats per character, on the same 256 fixed windows every time. Lower is better: 4.17 (ln 65) is blind guessing, and 1.6 means the model narrows each next character to about 5 likely choices (e1.6).
Noise
Three seeds (1, 2, 3) per arm. Seeds of the same arm differ by 0.005–0.02. The 256-window sample adds about ±0.018 to any single number, but that part is shared by every run, so comparisons between arms are much sharper than that.
Optimizers
AdamW (learning rate 1e-3, nanoGPT's setting) for the embeddings, the head and the norms in every arm. The four big matrices get either AdamW or Muon (learning rate 0.01, picked by a 3-point sweep).

2Three ideas you need

The 4-bit grid

The 4-bit format here is the value set of NVFP4, called E2M1: 1 sign bit, 2 exponent bits and 1 mantissa bit. It can hold exactly fifteen values: 0, ±0.5, ±1, ±1.5, ±2, ±3, ±4 and ±6. The spacing is uneven: 0.5 apart near zero, 2 apart at the top. Each block of 16 neighbouring weights gets its own scale, its largest |weight| divided by 6, so the biggest weight in the block lands exactly on ±6.

Random rounding

Without a float copy, every step has to land the weight back on the grid. Nearest rounding would undo any move smaller than half a gap, so the weights would freeze. Random (stochastic) rounding instead picks one of the two neighbours at random, weighted so the average lands in the right place. A value 30% of the way from 2 to 3 rounds up with probability 0.3. It is right on average, but each single result is off by up to a full gap.

Muon and Newton-Schulz

Muon keeps one momentum matrix per weight matrix: a running average of recent gradients (0.95 of the old value plus 0.05 of the new gradient each step). It does not step along the momentum directly. Any matrix stretches some directions more than others, and its singular values measure those stretches. Muon keeps the momentum's directions and sets every stretch to about 1. So the step says which way to move and ignores how much. The step size comes from the learning rate alone.

The operation is Newton-Schulz (NS): divide the matrix by its overall size, then apply this polynomial five times.

X ← a·X + b·(X Xᵀ)X + c·(X Xᵀ)²X (a, b, c) = (3.4445, −4.7750, 2.0315)

Where the rounding error goes in each arm

Each arm differs only in what happens to e, the gap between the weight the optimizer wanted and the grid value actually stored.

3Results

4Why dropping the float copy hurts

With a float copy (milestone 2), tiny moves pile up in the copy, and the 4-bit value changes only when the copy crosses a midpoint between two grid values. Without one (milestone 3), nothing remembers the tiny moves. The first chart shows how tiny they are: half of all weights move by less than this fraction of a grid gap per step.

Random rounding turns a 2% move into "jump a whole gap with probability 2%". On average that's right. But after N steps of size s on a grid with gap g, the wanted move is N·s while the random part is about √(N·s·g). Their ratio, √(N·s/g), falls as s falls. The noise doesn't shrink with the learning rate the way the signal does.

A side finding about noise. One re-run of milestone 3 differed from the committed run only in the order of one multiplication, a last-decimal change. It ended at 1.756 instead of 1.766. Random-rounding runs are chaotic: a tiny change sends them down a different path. So single-run differences below about 0.01 between no-copy arms mean nothing. Only the 3-seed means count.

5Why feeding the error into Muon's momentum fails

ECO (arXiv 2601.22101) starts from a simple idea. The rounding error e is exactly the part of the step that didn't happen, so put it into the momentum and the next steps will redo it. With plain momentum or Adam, the step equals a known number (or one known number per weight) times the momentum. Whatever you add to the momentum comes out of the next steps scaled by that known amount, so you can aim it exactly. ECO's rule (its equations 6–7) does just that.

momentum ← momentum + (1/η)(1 − 1/β)·e η = step size, β = 0.95

For Muon we used the same rule, with η replaced by Muon's step size per unit of momentum. That substitution is our adaptation, not the paper's. But Muon's step is NS(momentum), and NS is not "a number times". So we measured, every 25 steps for all 16 matrices, how the change NS makes, NS(M + d) − NS(M), compares with the injected term d.

Why about half? Picture the momentum's own directions as a set of axes, and split the error into three parts.

  • Stretch: makes one of the momentum's existing directions stronger or weaker. NS sets every strength to about 1, so this part is deleted.
  • Rotate: turns the existing directions into each other. It survives, divided by how strong those directions are.
  • Outside: points into directions the momentum doesn't use at all (only rectangular matrices have room for these). It survives, divided by the strength of the direction it attaches to.

The table reads left to right, as three losses stacked on top of each other. First, deleting the stretch part caps the cosine (first column). Then the survivors get boosted by different amounts, weak directions most, which bends them further (second column). Then Newton-Schulz with only five rounds is an approximate orthogonaliser, which costs a bit more (third column). The last column is the key check: a random error of the same size survives just as badly. Nothing special about rounding errors causes this. NS does it to any small change in the momentum.

6Why the bypass nearly works, and why it isn't masterless

Arm (c) keeps e in its own buffer and adds it back after Newton-Schulz, so nothing scrambles it. It tracks Muon's intended path almost as well as the float copy does (chart in section 4) and ends 0.027 behind milestone 2.

Arm (c) is a float copy in two pieces. The stored 4-bit weight plus the buffer equals the weight the optimizer wanted: ŵ + e = w̃. With a full-precision buffer and nearest rounding, arm (c) would be milestone 2, step for step. With a 16-bit buffer it is a 4 + 16 bit float copy: 6.75 bytes per weight against milestone 2's 8. It saves 1.25 bytes, and it isn't the method the question asks about.

Its remaining gap probably comes from random rounding in the forward pass. Because the buffer keeps pushing the weight back and forth, 31% of its codes flip every step, so the model always sees a randomly rounded version of the float copy. Milestone 2 sees a nearest-rounded, steady one. That's a hypothesis, untested. Section 9 lists the run that would check it.

7The control: ECO with Adam works

For Adam, each weight's step is its momentum times that weight's own step size. ECO divides the injected error by that same step size, so the error comes back out exactly. Muon has no per-weight step size: its step depends on the whole matrix at once, through Newton-Schulz. That is the whole difference.

The control doesn't rescue the question, though. Adam is 0.29 behind Muon on this model, so 4-bit Adam via ECO (1.928) is far worse than 4-bit Muon with a float copy (1.646).

9Where this leaves us

The problem, stated precisely. A Muon step moves each weight by 0.1–7% of one 4-bit grid gap. That fraction must be stored somewhere, or it gets replaced by random noise. Four places have been tried.

10Follow-up: spreading the rounding error over the matrix

The idea: random rounding makes an error e on every weight. Instead of leaving each error where it falls, push it onto neighbouring weights that feed the same output, so the errors cancel in the layer's output. GPTQ does exactly this, once, when it compresses a finished model. It rounds one input column at a time and corrects the later columns, weighted by how the layer's inputs move together, which is their covariance H = average(x xᵀ). Done each step, it would be "noise shaping", like dithering an image. The question is whether it lowers the noise when done every training step.

Before any training run, we measured it offline. We took three moments of a milestone 3 run and rounded the same intended weights in several ways, three times each. Then we measured how much noise reached each layer's output on held-out text, relative to the output change the step intended. All methods use random rounding, so all are right on average, and lower noise is strictly better.

Method (noise relative to plain random rounding; 1 = same, below 1 = better)step 250step 1000step 1900

Every method made the noise worse, and worse later in training. Theory said otherwise: judged by the inputs' covariance alone, the full method should cut the noise to 0.16–0.39×. Two test runs found the cause.

  • On an evenly spaced grid, spreading works (0.3–0.5×, close to theory). But plain random rounding on that grid is already 4.5× noisier than on the 4-bit float grid, which keeps its fine 0.5 steps near zero, where most weights sit.
  • The real reason is that the target is always next to a grid point. Without a float copy, the weight we want is the stored grid value plus a tiny step (0.1–7% of a gap). Random rounding of such a value is nearly exact: it stays put with high probability, and its noise per weight is only about step × gap. Spreading hands every later weight a correction about one rounding error in size, which moves it to the middle of a gap, where random rounding is noisiest (gap²/4). The smaller the steps get, the worse the trade.
  • Spreading the error of nearest rounding instead (no randomness) changes nothing at step 1000. The step-sized errors never add up to a flip, which is milestone 2's freeze again.

Milestone 5 then trained the cheap version anyway, with its pass line written first: spread only inside each block of 16, at 0.29 extra bytes per weight. That was a check that the offline measurement predicts training, not a hope.

What this says about any "redistribute the error" scheme. Without a float copy, random rounding's noise adds up to roughly (total distance the weights travel) × (grid gap). Moving the error to other weights within one step can't shrink that, because every weight is already as close to the grid as it will ever be. Moving it to later steps is milestone 4, and Newton-Schulz scrambles it. What's left is shortening the path (fewer back-and-forth moves), shrinking the gap (more bits, or a better scale), or storing the fraction somewhere (a copy, by another name).

11Follow-up: FP8 block scales

Milestone 3 stores each block's scale in fp32 and recomputes it every step. So each block's largest weight always lands exactly on ±6, and in effect trains at full precision. The ablation stores scales as NVFP4 does: an 8-bit float per block of 16, under one fp32 scale per matrix.

Seedm3, fp32 scalesFP8 scalesdifference

FP8 scales cost about 0.008, the size of the seed-to-seed spread, and save 0.19 bytes per weight. The hidden full-precision weight in each block explains little of milestone 3's result. Activations are a separate question: the scales here belong to the stored weights. A 4-bit activation format would need its own scales, computed fresh for every input, and none of these runs quantise activations.

12Follow-up: training 5× longer, floors and speed

Do the arms just descend at different speeds, or do they level off at different floors? The same model was trained for 10,000 steps instead of 2000 on a free T4 GPU, using 16-bit arithmetic (so the numbers are not comparable digit for digit with the CPU runs). Each arm's peak learning rate was re-picked on seed 1 from three values: AdamW 0.002, Muon 0.005. As planned in advance, m3 used Muon's and ECO-Adam used AdamW's. Then each ran seeds 1–3. 10,000 steps is 7.7 million characters, about 7.6 passes over the training text.

The five questions were written down before the runs.

    Floors against speed. On this model, Muon's big lead at 2000 steps is mostly speed: at 10,000 steps AdamW is only 0.034 behind. m3's problem is a floor. It is faster than AdamW early on, then levels off about 0.13 higher. That fits the noise argument: random rounding's noise grows with the total distance the weights travel, so the longer the run, the more it costs. For single-pass training on a huge dataset, the question is which arm is lowest after a fixed number of new characters. That favours speed, but these runs repeat the same small text 7.6 times, so they don't answer it. A single-pass test needs a bigger text (for example, the 100 MB enwik8), where no character is seen twice.

    13What this does not show

    • One small model (0.8M weights, 2000 steps) on one small dataset, on the CPU preset. The orderings may change at the GPU preset (6 layers, width 384, 5000 steps) or at real scale.
    • Muon's learning rate was tuned at full precision only. The 4-bit arms might prefer a different one, and the best value (0.01) was the smallest tried.
    • Block scales are fp32 in milestones 2–5. Storing them in FP8 as NVFP4 does cost m3 only +0.008 (section 11). No arm quantises activations.
    • Arm (b) uses our own Muon version of ECO's step size. A different choice would inject a different amount. Because a random error survives just as badly, a different amount is unlikely to fix the scrambling.
    • Survival was measured on the plain momentum, not on the Nesterov mix Muon actually orthogonalises, and for one perturbation at a time.
    • ECO's exact form, which also uses the previous step's error, was not tried. Nor was nearest rounding with feedback.
    • AdamW was not retuned at 2000 steps. At 10,000 steps with a re-picked learning rate, Muon's lead shrinks to 0.034 (section 12), so most of the 0.29 was speed.
    • The long runs used 16-bit arithmetic on a T4 and read the same text 7.6 times. Single-pass training on a large text is untested. m3's learning rate there was Muon's, fixed in advance. The higher learning rates in section 12 are one seed each.
    • Error spreading was tested within one step only, and without any float remainder.
